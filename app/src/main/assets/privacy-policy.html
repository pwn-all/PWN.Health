PWN.Health — Privacy Policy

Effective date: 26 November 2026 · App package: com.pwnall.health · App version 2.0.1

The short version: PWN.Health does not have the Android INTERNET permission. It opens no network connection of its own, and there are no servers, no accounts, no analytics, no advertising and no trackers of any kind — nothing about you is sent to us while you use the app. Data can still leave the app, and always onto your own phone, because you set it up that way: it can be handed as a file to an app you pick — an export, a report, a photograph, a daily copy into a folder you chose — and, if you connect Health Connect, your records are exchanged with it automatically from then on, every time you open the app. Section 8 lists every one of those routes, because a promise that stops at the app's own process is only half the answer.

2. What the app records

Everything below is kept in one database in the app's private storage, which no other app on the phone can read. What is present depends on which features you use, with a floor everyone has: the profile onboarding asks for before the app shows you anything — sex, weight, height and your full date of birth — and the weight and height it logs as your first measurement. Past that floor, a person who only counts steps has a database of steps, plus the day's climb and average air pressure if the phone has a barometer.

  • Profile: sex, weight, height, your full date of birth, unit system, activity level, daily step goal, whether you are maintaining, losing or gaining weight, whether you have told the app about food allergies (the answer itself, which may be that you have never been tested, or nothing at all if you skipped the question), and whether automatic sleep detection and reminders are switched on. We keep the whole date of birth rather than the year because it decides whether the app offers alcohol, tobacco or intimate logging at all (see §14), and a year alone would open those up to eleven months early.
  • Activity: steps kept both as a daily total and hour by hour, the time of your most recent step, floors climbed, the day's average air pressure — built from a reading taken roughly every ten minutes and kept only as that day's running total and sample count, never as the separate readings — workouts with their sets, repetitions, loads and rests, where the exercises of one session in Freestyle or from a template carry that session's name and number; workout templates, each with its exercises, their sets and supersets, the rests between them, the days of the week you train it, a short description and a photograph if you add one (§3); the dates you plan a template for, under its name, which go when you delete the template; and breathing sessions.
  • Which dates you opened the app: one row per date, nothing else — no time, no duration, no screen. It exists so that the level and XP figure on the home screen can count an opening streak, and it is used for nothing else. It travels in a backup file and it goes with Clear data, like the rest.
  • Recorded routes: while a recording runs, one point roughly every two seconds. Each point holds the coordinates, how accurate the phone said they were, height above sea level, the air pressure and temperature the phone measured there, your speed, and whether the app judged that moment to be walking, running, riding or standing still. Points the app rejected as unreliable are kept beside the accepted ones, so a recording can be argued with later. A recording you pause stores no point until you resume it; the route keeps how long it stood paused in all, and marks the first point after each pause as the start of a new stretch. A route is given a name when its recording starts, picked at random from a list in the app, and it may hold photographs you took while it recorded, each with the spot it was taken from (§3).
  • Nutrition: meals with calories, protein, fat, carbohydrate, fibre, sugar, saturated fat, sodium and cholesterol; water, caffeine, alcohol by ethanol content, and tobacco. Saved dishes and favourite foods you keep for re-use.
  • Health measurements: heart rate, heart-rate variability worked out from the beat-to-beat intervals a chest strap reports, the resting heart rate and heart-rate variability a watch or a ring wrote to Health Connect, blood pressure, blood sugar, body temperature, oxygen saturation, sleep, body composition, the waist, hip and chest measurements you take with a tape, and readings taken from Bluetooth devices you connect (see §7). A reading from an instrument is stored with the whole of what the instrument reported, which can be more than the app displays — a body-composition scale's impedance figures, a glucose meter's sample site, a thermometer's measurement site.
  • Mood, as a five-point self-report, from which the app works out a wellbeing score on the scale the WHO-5 index uses; when you note it in a chat, also what you picked it was about, from a short list.
  • Menstrual-cycle records: the first and last day of a period, flow, spotting, ovulation-test results and cervical-mucus appearance, from which the app estimates your average cycle length and the likely date of your next period.
  • Medical records you keep yourself: medicines with their dose, schedule and food instruction, and a photograph of the box if you take one, doses you mark as taken — on the Medicine tab, from a reminder, a widget or in a chat — and doses you record in a chat that belong to no course, doctors' appointments with the specialty, name and place you typed, routine check-ups, and laboratory results — thirty-six markers across blood sugar, lipids, kidneys, liver, blood count, inflammation, thyroid, vitamins and minerals, and urine, most of them compared with a published reference band, together with what you type about the sample itself: whether it was serum, plasma, whole blood, capillary blood or urine — or saliva, on a result saved when the app still offered it — which laboratory or analyser reported it, and the reference range printed on the sheet. An allergy panel is kept as a result of its own: each allergen read off the sheet, by its code and the name printed beside it, with its specific IgE and its class, together with the sheet itself. An ultrasound, an MRI or another examination is kept as one file or photograph, with the date of the examination and the note you typed; the app never reads what is in it.
  • The medical history you write down yourself: diagnoses, operations and procedures, vaccinations, pregnancy status and contraception method, each with the dates and the note you typed. The app treats all of it as your own statement — it checks nothing, grades nothing and attaches no diagnostic codes of its own.
  • Existing files you attach to a medicine, an appointment, a lab result, an examination or a chat — a photographed prescription, a scan, a letter. The app does not copy these in. It remembers where the file is and takes Android's permission to read it the moment you pick it, so the file stays in whichever app you picked it from. The permission is given up once no saved entry names the file any more: at once when you delete the lab result, allergy panel or examination it is on, and otherwise at a later fresh start of the app, never within an hour of the pick — not when Android brings it back with its screens as you left them. That covers a file you take off an entry and save, one on an entry you delete, and one you picked into a form you then left unsaved or replaced with another. On a form, the × beside a file only changes the form: leave it unsaved and the entry keeps the file, and the permission with it; a chat saves the × at once, as it saves everything written in it. While a copy of the database the app has set aside is on the phone (§11), that start lets nothing go.
  • Intimate and bodily records: sexual acts, marked as protected or unprotected, and masturbation as a separate record; on either you can tick symptoms from a fixed list. Visits to the toilet as a bowel movement or a urination, each of which can carry its own symptom ticks. Kept for the same reason as everything else — because you chose to record them.
  • Morning check-ins, on a profile set to male and only from the intimate screen: whether you woke with an erection — firm, partly, none, or that you did not notice — the time you woke, whether that time came from your sleep record or you set it, and, if you say, what woke you. These are intimate records in the same sense as the ones above, and the app treats them as such: they never appear on the home screen, a widget or a notification.
  • Pain entries: where it hurt, painted on a drawing of the body, with the areas the painting covers; up to two words for how it felt; a score from 1, mild, to 10, unbearable; when it started and when you first noticed it; whether it has gone and how long it lasted; whether you took a painkiller for it; when you say an area marked again is the same pain as one you logged in the month before, the moment that earlier pain was first logged; and “no pain today” when you say so.
  • Chats: everything you write in a chat, word for word, even when you leave before answering anything; the topics you pick — something in the body, such as dizziness or a fever, a feeling, such as worry or low mood, a pain, your mood, a medicine you took or one a doctor prescribed — and your answers to the chat's questions, including any answer to its questions about thoughts of suicide or self-harm, and a pain you painted on its body map before you sent it; where the chat's model read a message, what it made of it (§2.2); a snapshot of the journal around that moment, such as your last meal, your steps and your sleep; any files you attach to it; and, for each entry the chat made for you or you added from one of its cards, its kind and title, the moment it was saved, and a short fingerprint of it — or, for one added from a card, its number in the journal and the amount — so the chat can show it again and open it; and the earlier state of a mood or a pain entry it changed for you, so Undo can put it back — kept until you leave the chat once it is over, or else until you next open it to read. Each topic of a chat is also sorted into a few categories the app works out from your answers on the phone: roughly where it was felt, how long it has gone on, whether it comes back, how much it gets in the way, how urgent the answers were, and which topics your words named. These categories are not a diagnosis, and nothing in the app shows them; they are kept with the chat and, for a symptom, in its entry too, and so they travel wherever the chat and that entry do. A chat is kept apart from your journal and counts for nothing in it. What it records for you, each from your own taps on its answers — a symptom entry, a pain entry, a mood, a dose marked as taken, a medicine taken without a course, a new course of medicine with its reminders — is an ordinary entry of that kind, as if you had made it on its own page; a symptom entry also names the chat it came from, and a course added in a chat rings its dose reminders like any other. An answer to one of its questions about safety — thoughts of suicide or self-harm, an emergency, or having taken more of a medicine than it says — given while the chat is about something other than a symptom is kept in a symptom entry of its own, so that it is in your journal as well as in the chat. When the chat finds an entry in what you wrote — a drink, a meal, a workout, a night's sleep — it offers it on a card: Add saves water, a cup of coffee or tea, or a meal of one food from the app's catalogue straight from the card — a meal with the message you wrote as its note, which stays in your journal even if you delete the chat — and opens any other entry's own page filled in from your message, where it is saved only if you save it there.
  • Work sessions: when you pressed Start, Pause, Resume and Stop on the work timer or its widget, sessions you typed in afterwards, and the two settings the Work screen keeps — your usual days off and where a long day starts. While the timer runs, its state is kept in the app's own settings, not in the database, until you press Stop.
  • A workplace, only if you save one for the Work screen's Start by location: one point — the latitude and longitude where you stood when you saved it, how accurate Android said that position was (a point is saved only when that is within 100 metres), and when you saved it — with the radius you set around it. It is kept in the app's own settings, not in the database, so it is not in a backup file you export, and the database-encryption switch does not cover it; a move to a new phone carries the app's settings, and this with them. Between two checks the app also keeps when it last looked and when it looks next, whether it started the session now running, when a check last found you there, how many checks since have found you away, the day you last took the timer over from it, and which session it last stopped and when, for that stop's Undo. No position from a check is ever kept. Removing the workplace deletes all of it.
  • Free text you type: a note on almost any entry, titles for meals and workouts, the name and description of a workout template, and the doctor and clinic names you have entered before, which the app offers back to you as suggestions. The app does not read these, with one exception: what you write in a chat, the answers you type into it, and the note on one, are searched on the phone for a few words — of suicide, of an emergency, and of the chat's own topics, such as a part of the body, a mood, or the name of a medicine, matched against your own medicines and a short list of common painkillers and antidepressants built into the app — so the chat can ask about them or offer them for you to tap. Where the app carries the chat's model, the same messages, answers and note are also read by that model, on the phone (§2.2). No entry is made from those words alone; the topics they named are kept only among the categories above, and what the model made of a message only with the chat. Whatever you put in a note is in the database.
  • Provenance: for anything that did not start in this app — a record that arrived through Health Connect, or a row read out of a file you imported — where it came from and the identifier it came under, so the Data sources screen can tell you which figure the app is believing, and so that importing the same thing twice does not duplicate it. Any entry you correct also keeps the moment you last changed it; for one that came through Health Connect, that moment is what tells the next exchange to leave your correction standing.

A history can also come in from a file another app exported, through the Import from other apps page. The health exports of Apple Health, Fitbit and Google Health, Garmin Connect, Samsung Health, Withings, Oura, Strava, Nike Run Club, MyFitnessPal, Cronometer and Flo are read — the file or the archive that app wrote, as far as this app knows its parts — into steps, weighings and body composition, vital signs, sleep, workouts, food, water, caffeine and menstrual periods; the training logs of Hevy, Strong, FitNotes and Lyfta become workouts in your journal. wger exports no training log as a file at all, only your list of weighings, and those are read here as measurements. This needs no permission: Android's own file picker hands over the one file you chose, and nothing else on the phone is readable through it. The file is copied into the app's private cache while the import runs and read from there — an archive is read in memory, never unpacked onto the phone — and the copy is deleted when the import ends, or, for an import cut off by Android closing the app halfway, at a later fresh start of the app. What is kept is the rows, each stamped with the name of the app the file came from, so opening the same export a second time adds nothing; the file's own name is not kept.

The world map behind a recorded route is part of the app, not a map service: its land and water, relief, forests and towns are files inside the installed package. No tile is fetched, and no coordinate of yours is ever sent to a map provider, because there is nothing to send it with. The food catalogue the app searches is the same — a table shipped inside the app, drawn from the United States Department of Agriculture's public data. Searching it sends nothing anywhere. So is the list of clinics and hospitals an appointment's Place page suggests: it is drawn from official registers and open data on places of care in Czechia, Ukraine and the United Arab Emirates (the page's “?” says which places it holds), and searching it or sorting it by distance from you happens on the phone.

Pain entries, chats, symptom entries, work sessions and morning check-ins are never written to Health Connect, which has no record for any of them. That does not mean they stay on the phone: like everything above, they are in a backup you make, in the automatic daily copy if you switch it on and in a move to a new phone, and pain entries, work sessions and morning check-ins are on a report for a doctor if you tick them; chats and symptom entries are not on the report. §8 sets out each of those routes.

Almost all of this is data concerning health, and European law treats that whole class as special-category data — not only the parts of it that look most delicate. The intimate records, morning check-ins among them, are special-category on a second count, as data about your sex life. §13 says on what basis we process it.

2.1 The app's own identifiers

The app gives itself a random name — an installation identifier, tied to nothing about you or your phone — the first time it needs one, which is when it first writes or reads a backup or produces a diagnostics report, and a new one the same way after its database has had to start over. Android's transfer to a new phone does not carry it: the app there gives itself a new one. It exists so that backup files can be told apart, and so that two bug reports from one phone can be recognised as coming from one phone. It is written into the header of every backup file the app writes, and it appears in the diagnostics report if you send us one. The app reads no advertising identifier, no Android ID and no hardware serial.

The records the app publishes to Health Connect carry a second random name, eight characters long and tied to nothing about you or your phone either. It stands inside each record's own identifier and names the journal the record was written from, so that a reinstalled app, whose journal numbers its entries from 1 again, does not write over what the installation before it published. Health Connect may show a record's identifier to any app you allow to read that kind of record. The name is made anew after a reinstall, after Clear data and after the database has had to start over. Android's device-to-device transfer carries it to a new phone with the settings, and a new one is made there if the database that came with it cannot be opened.

2.2 The chat's model

The chat can also read what you write with a small AI model that ships inside the app and runs on the phone, with no network. Not every version of the app carries one: while one reads your messages, a line under the chat's title says so, and without that line what you write is read by the chat's word lists alone. The model is a reader, not a writer: it never words a sentence of its own.

  • What it reads: only the message you send — each one once, as you send it, and likewise an answer you type and the note — together with where the chat stands: at its start, between topics, at a question (which one, and of which topic) or at the note after the end. It does not read your journal, your medicines, your profile or anything else the app holds.
  • What it does with it: it only chooses among what the chat already has — which of its fixed topics to offer as buttons, and in which order; which button to mark as what your words said, or to offer for a single tap; where a scale's pointer starts; and which of the chat's fixed questions about safety to add: about thoughts of suicide or self-harm, someone not responding, breathing, chest pain, a stroke, vomiting blood or too much of a medicine. It can add one of those questions, never remove, reword or reorder one: the questions the app's word lists bring are asked as before, and whether the chat says to get help comes from your answers and the app's fixed rules. Nothing it reads answers a question or makes an entry: only your tap does. In a language its reading has not yet passed the app's own checks in, it only adds those questions about safety, and everything else comes from the word lists.
  • It can be wrong: it may offer a topic you did not mean, miss one you did, or ask a question about safety you did not need. That is why it only offers, and why the questions about danger that the app's word lists find are asked whatever it reads.
  • What is kept: with each message the chat keeps the model's version and the decisions taken from what it read — the questions it added, the topics it offered, whether you wrote of feeling unwell or about someone else, and what it read your words to say of a pain (where and on which side, when it started and when you noticed it, its kind, whether it is still there or the same as before, its warning signs, how bad or how sudden it was, a number out of ten, a painkiller taken) and of a medicine (its name as you wrote it, what you said of it, an amount, a time of day) — or why it kept to the word lists for that message, such as running out of time. None of the model's probabilities is kept, and a message is never read again: the chat is drawn from these decisions each time you open it. They are kept in the chat, in the app's database, and so they go wherever the chat goes: into a backup you make, into the automatic daily copy if you switch it on, and to a new phone in a move (§8). Like the chat, they are never written to Health Connect and are not on a report for a doctor. Deleting the chat deletes them from the app; a backup written before that still holds them.
  • What it sends, and what it learns: it sends nothing anywhere — it runs inside the app, which has no internet permission (§4). It was trained before it was put into the app, never on anything written in the app, and it learns nothing from what you write, on the phone or anywhere else: the app sends us none of it (§9).

3. Photographs

The app has no camera permission and never opens a camera. When you photograph a dish or a nutrition label, PWN.Health asks your own camera app to take the picture and hands it one file inside PWN.Health's private cache to write into; on a phone with no camera app, the same button opens Android's own photo picker so you can choose an existing picture. That camera app is separate software with its own permissions and its own privacy policy, and many camera apps keep a copy of what they take in your gallery. The app also has no photo-library permission: where a picture is picked rather than taken, Android's picker shows PWN.Health only the files you chose. Laboratory imports can include several photographs or PDFs at once, and recognised readings fill a form for you to review before saving. A PDF is copied into the app's private cache while it is read and deleted when the reading ends — or, if Android closed the app in the middle of it, at a fresh start of the app at least an hour later; one locked with a password is opened with the password you type, which is used for that reading and never stored. Recognition runs entirely on the device.

The app keeps photographs under six different rules:

  • Dish and nutrition-label photographs are not kept. Each is written under one fixed name in the app's private cache, read by models that ship inside the app and run on the device, and deleted when the reading is accepted or you leave the page; the next photograph overwrites the last. They are never written to your gallery or to any shared folder, and they are excluded from every Android backup path. The reading itself — what the label said — is what is kept.
  • Laboratory camera photographs are kept. These are the shots you take of a lab sheet, an allergy panel or an examination's document. The camera app writes each shot into a separate file in PWN.Health's private files directory. A shot larger than a printed page needs — more than 3,508 pixels on its longer side — is then written again at that size, and the copy carries none of the details the camera app put into the file; a smaller one is kept as the camera app wrote it, with whatever details that app put into it — the phone's make and model, the moment it was taken and, if the camera app records it, the place — and goes so into a backup file and a report's archive (§8.1). The photograph is attached to the lab form automatically; later shots do not overwrite it. Tapping one among a result's attachments hands that one file to whatever app your phone views pictures with. Deleting the result deletes its photograph, unless another saved result names the same shot. One no saved result names — taken off the form, or on a form you left unsaved — is deleted at a later fresh start of the app once it is an hour old, except while a copy of the database the app has set aside is on the phone (§11). Every such photograph a saved result still names is included whole inside every backup file, including the automatic daily copy, and all of them are carried by Android's device-to-device transfer. They are excluded from Android cloud backup and are not covered by the database-encryption switch. Existing photos and PDFs selected through the file picker remain in their original provider and are not copied in; a backup carries only where they are.
  • Progress photographs are kept. These are pictures of yourself, taken alongside a weigh-in, or picked from your gallery, if you choose to. A picked one is copied, and the original stays where it is. Besides the picture itself and which way up it goes, the app reads one thing from the original file: the date and time it says it was taken, which the copy keeps, to the second, as its own — it is filed under that day, its file is named after that moment, and the moment travels with it in backups. Each is shrunk to 1 280 pixels on its longer edge and written as a new picture, which carries none of the original file's other details, into the app's private files directory, which no gallery indexes and no other app can read. They are included whole inside every backup file, including the automatic daily copy; they are carried to a new phone by Android's device-to-device transfer; and one button hands all of them at once to an app you choose. The × on a thumbnail deletes the picture, file and all. They are not covered by the database-encryption switch, which protects the database file only.
  • Medicine photographs are kept. These are pictures of a medicine's box or blister, taken with the camera button beside its name if you choose to — or, on a phone without a camera app, picked from your gallery, in which case a copy is made and the original stays where it is. The picture is shrunk to 1 280 pixels on its longer edge at once, and the camera's full-size shot is deleted. Until you save the course, the shrunk copy waits under one fixed name in the app's private cache, and the next photograph replaces it; saving moves it into the app's private files directory, which no gallery indexes and no other app can read. It is shown beside the medicine's name; tapping it hands that one file to whatever app your phone views pictures with. It is included whole inside every backup file, including the automatic daily copy, and carried to a new phone by Android's device-to-device transfer. Removing or retaking it and saving the course, or deleting the course, deletes the old file. It is not covered by the database-encryption switch.
  • Walk photographs are kept. These are pictures you take while a route is recording or paused, if you choose to; your own camera app takes them. Each is shrunk to no more than 1 920 pixels on its longer edge and written into the app's private files directory, which no gallery indexes and no other app can read. With it the app stores when it was taken and the spot it was taken from: the position the route recorded nearest that moment, with its height and how accurate it was, when the route has one within a minute of it — or the phone's own position from that minute, if it is no vaguer than about fifty metres, when the route has none or that one is nearer the moment (as during a pause, which records no positions); the compass direction the camera faced, when the phone could tell it, with the sensor's own estimate of how far off it may be; how far the camera tilted up or down; and how the phone was held. A picture with a position is marked on the walk's map under the same number as its thumbnail. Tapping one hands that one file to whatever app your phone views pictures with. They are included whole inside every backup file, including the automatic daily copy, and carried to a new phone by Android's device-to-device transfer. The × beside one on the walk page deletes the picture, file and all. They are not covered by the database-encryption switch.
  • Workout template photographs are kept. This is the picture you give a workout template, if you choose to: taken by your camera app or picked from your gallery, in which case a copy is made and the original stays where it is. It is shrunk to 1 280 pixels on its longer edge and written as a new picture, which carries none of the original file's details. Until you save the template it waits under one fixed name in the app's private cache, and the next picture replaces it; saving moves it into the app's private files directory, which no gallery indexes and no other app can read. It is included whole inside every backup file, including the automatic daily copy, carried to a new phone by Android's device-to-device transfer, and put inside the file when you share the template (§8.1). Removing or replacing it and saving the template, or deleting the template, deletes the old file. It is not covered by the database-encryption switch.

Every model that reads a photograph runs on the phone. PWN.Health cannot upload a photograph — it has no internet permission — but it can hand one to an app you pick, and that app may upload it.

4. Permissions

These are the permissions in the app that ships. Nothing here is on by default that Android would otherwise ask you about: every runtime permission below is one you are asked for and may refuse, and refusing one costs you only the feature it belongs to. Onboarding offers these permissions before Health Connect: step counting, notifications, nearby devices and route location, plus Android's settings for exact alarms, lock-screen alarms and optional Usage Access. Each is optional and can be changed later. Granting Usage Access alone does not turn on sleep estimation; its separate switch remains off until you enable it — or until you choose On waking for a medicine's doses on the medicine's page: such a dose is timed by that estimate, so choosing it asks for Usage Access where it is missing and then switches the estimate on.

5. Sensors, and what runs when you are not looking

Some of what the app measures needs no Android permission, so Android never asks you about it. That makes this section the only place you would learn of it.

  • While step counting is on, the accelerometer runs at twenty-five readings a second to tell walking from a car, a train or a phone being shaken. The readings themselves are held for about twelve seconds and never stored; only the decision they lead to is. There is one exception, and you have to switch it on yourself: the sensor view, behind seven taps on the version line in About the app, can record a trace. While that recording runs, every reading is kept in memory — up to about two hours of them — until you either discard it or write it to a file of your choosing, and the processor is kept awake meanwhile so that the readings go on with the screen off. The same view can open such a file again, to count the repetitions in it; that reads the file into memory and stores nothing. Nothing sends it anywhere.
  • The barometer runs alongside it, to count floors climbed and to fold one air-pressure reading every ten minutes into that day's average.
  • The gyroscope and the accelerometer run only between Start and Stop, when you ask the app to count repetitions of an exercise — one or the other, whichever that exercise needs, at fifty readings a second: the gyroscope for squats, lunges, push-ups and the glute bridge, and the accelerometer for jump rope, jumping jacks, burpees, calf raises, pull-ups and leg raises on the bars, and, in the form for an earlier day, for stair climbing. In a workout from a template or in Freestyle, a set of walking, running, hiking or stairs is counted by the step counter instead, and the floors of a climb by the barometer, again only between Start and Stop. While a set is counted in a workout from a template or in Freestyle, the app keeps the processor awake so that the sensors go on with the screen locked in a pocket (§4); the form for an earlier day does not. The readings of a set are held in memory while it is open on screen, so the count can be recomputed, and are never stored; only the count you keep is — the repetitions, jumps or steps, and a climb's floors. Apart from that, the gyroscope runs only inside the rotation sensor below.
  • The thermometer, on phones that have one, runs only while a route records.
  • The rotation sensor — which Android builds from the gyroscope, the accelerometer and, on a phone that has one, the compass — is read for two things. While the track screen or a recorded walk's page is open, with the app in front, it turns the map with the phone, as a compass card turns, and points the arrow on your position, where there is one, the way the phone faces. To point at true rather than magnetic north, the app works out the magnetic declination at the walk's first point, or else at your position, from a model built into Android, on the phone. The heading is only drawn, never stored, and the sensor is let go when you leave the page or the app goes to the background. The second is a walk photograph taken while a route records or stands paused: from the moment you tap the camera button until the photograph comes back, and for five minutes at most, long enough to note which way the camera faced, how far it was tilted and how the phone was held (§3). Nothing else in the app reads it.
  • The app can estimate a pulse from the accelerometer: with the phone resting on your chest it listens for thirty to sixty seconds and reads a heart rate from the movement each beat makes. It runs only when you ask for it, and it offers the number for you to correct before it is saved. It is an estimate from motion — not an oximeter, and not an ECG.
  • Automatic sleep detection is off by default and needs two separate switches: the one in the app, and Android's Usage access, which only you can grant from Android Settings. When it is on, each time you open the app it asks Android about phone activity across the last seven nights and looks for the longest untouched gap. Android hands back a full event log that includes which apps were in the foreground; the app discards all of that immediately, keeping only each event's type and the moment it happened, and stores nothing but the estimated sleep. No list of the apps you use is ever read into the app's memory beyond that instant, and none of it is stored. You can revoke usage access in Android Settings at any time.
  • Screen time uses the same Usage access, and is the one place the app does read which apps were in front — but only for a fixed list of about sixty social, video, messenger and game apps built into it (TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Telegram, Roblox and the like). As the log is read, every other app is stripped of its name on the spot: that some app came forward is kept, because it ends a sitting, and which app it was is never held. From the listed apps it works out, for the last seven days, how long each was open and the longest sitting without a break. That is shown and then dropped: nothing about it goes into the journal, the backup or Health Connect, and the app keeps only your two reminder settings and, for each reminder, the sitting or the day it last spoke for and how many times. With a reminder switched on, the app wakes every fifteen minutes to read the log from the start of yesterday — today's figures, and a sitting that began the evening before — and decide whether to remind you; with both off it does not wake for this at all.
  • While reminders are on, the app wakes about once an hour to look at the day's steps, whether you have logged anything, whether you have eaten or drunk, whether a dose is due and whether a workout is planned for the day. If you keep a medicine cabinet it also wakes at the minute each dose is due, and again at intervals across the four hours after it while the dose stays unmarked, after which it lets that dose go; and once at midnight, whether anything is due or not, to work out the next day's times. Those wakes are exact where Android has granted the exact-alarm permission of §4, and otherwise fall at a moment Android picks. That clock is put back after a restart, after the app updates, and after the phone's time or time zone changes, and it is cleared when you switch reminders off. It can also offer a single note about your average daily steps, at most once a week — and to work out whether there is anything to say, it reads your whole day history and your whole journal on one of those hourly wakes, and reads them again on the next one until such a note has actually been shown, which for someone already walking enough may be never. All of it is worked out on the phone.
  • With the Work screen's Start by location switched on, the app wakes every ten minutes inside that day's window, as §4 describes — and, when you have granted Usage access, every ten minutes before it too, from five in the morning or from an hour before your usual start, until it sees that you are up. Each wake reads your recent work sessions and the day's sleep record, and — when you have granted Usage access — asks Android for the phone's activity since the evening before, the same reading the morning check-in makes, keeping only the moment you woke. Only when a check could start or stop the timer does it take a position. Switching it off, or removing the workplace, ends the wakes.

6. Health Connect

Health Connect is Android's own store, which apps on your phone use to share health records with each other. Connecting to it is optional, and the app works fully without it.

PWN.Health asks for a large set of Health Connect permissions — around thirty kinds of record, most of which it both reads and writes. Two it only reads: the resting heart rate and the heart-rate variability a watch or a ring works out, usually overnight, which the app shows on the vitals screen beside your own four-week median and never computes or writes itself. They include intimate categories: menstrual periods and flow, intermenstrual bleeding, ovulation tests, cervical mucus, and sexual activity. Those are offered so that a cycle or an intimate record you already keep in another app is not lost when you switch. The whole set is requested in one of Android's own screens, and you approve or refuse each type individually there; refusing one does not break the others, and you may approve none of them. That full request is made at the end of the first-run questions, after the height and weight wheels, and again from the Health Connect card on the Import from other apps page whenever you ask for it. Sexual activity is not asked for at all if the birth date you gave puts you under sixteen: the app does not show you that screen, so it does not ask for the record behind it. Declining the request, in whole or in part, changes nothing else — the app goes on counting what the phone itself counts.

Three things about this exchange are worth being plain about:

  • It is automatic once you have allowed it. The app is not asking each time. Once any permission stands, records are exchanged whenever you open the app or return to it — at most once every five minutes — and a weigh-in, a height, a pulse, a blood-pressure, oxygen, temperature or blood-sugar reading, a night's sleep, a workout, a drink or a meal you save is published within seconds. Every other kind — cycle and intimate entries, breathing sessions, planned workouts, what a workout measured — waits for the next exchange, as does a deletion (§11). The app offers no switch of its own to stop this: you stop it by revoking the permissions in Health Connect's own settings, which you can do at any moment.
  • Some free text goes with it. A note on a sleep entry, a workout's title and note, a meal's name, and the name of a workout template you have planned for a date are published as part of those records, and any app you have authorised to read that type can read them. Notes on every other kind of entry stay in the app. Of the intimate records, only the fact that an act happened and whether protection was used is exchanged — never the note. Morning check-ins, pain entries and work sessions are not exchanged at all.
  • Route coordinates are never written to Health Connect. Figures worked out from a route may be — the distance, the climb, a thinned series of speeds — but never the places you were.

Records that arrive this way are stored with the name of the app that wrote them, which the Data sources screen shows you. To turn a package name into the name you know an app by, PWN.Health asks the package manager on your own phone; that lookup stays on the device.

Deletion across Health Connect has limits, most of them Health Connect's rather than ours, and §11 sets them out.

7. Bluetooth devices

The app reads scales, heart-rate straps, blood-pressure monitors, pulse oximeters, glucose meters, thermometers, smart rings, a Bangle.js 2 watch running PWN.Health's own watch app, cycling power and speed sensors, running foot pods, treadmills, rowing and ski ergometers, and gym bikes. Most are connected to; some — certain scales and gym-bike consoles — simply broadcast their reading, and the app reads it out of the air without connecting or pairing at all.

The app stores what the device reported and enough about the device to recognise it again: not its Bluetooth address, but a fingerprint of it. A smart ring's fingerprint comes out the same on any phone, so that a backup restored on a new one goes on adding to the days the ring has already counted, and anyone with a copy of your data could work the ring's address back out of it. Every other device's is mixed with a secret of this installation, which the app's backup files leave out. Its Bluetooth traffic runs in one direction as far as your records are concerned: no measurement of yours is ever sent to a device. What the app does send are the requests a device needs before it will hand anything over — “send me your stored readings”, “what is your battery level” — and, where a device demands it, a pairing request.

That pairing is the one thing that outlives the app. Android stores it, not us, and clearing the app's data or uninstalling the app does not remove it. To remove it, forget the device in Android Settings → Connected devices.

8. When data leaves the device

The app cannot open a network connection. But data does leave the phone, and there are two different kinds of route: the ones you take deliberately, one file at a time, and the ones that keep running once you have switched them on.

8.1 Copies you make, one at a time

Each of these happens when you ask for it. Some are saved to a location you pick in Android's file picker; others are handed to an app you pick from Android's share sheet — and from that moment the app that received it holds its own copy, under its own privacy policy, not ours.

  • a full backup, containing everything you have recorded, with your progress, medicine, walk and workout-template photographs, and the laboratory camera photographs your results still name, inside the same file. The app's settings are not in it — its colours, the Work screen's days off, long day and workplace, and the other choices it keeps as settings rather than as records — so a phone restored from the file asks for them again; a move to a new phone carries them. You can put a password on it, and then it is written as AES-256-GCM in chunks under a key derived from your password with PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 and a random salt. The key is never stored — it is derived again from the password when you import — so a forgotten password cannot be recovered, by you or by us, and the file stays sealed. Without a password the export is plain text that anything can read;
  • a route, as GPX, GeoJSON or CSV — saved through the file picker or, from the walk page, shared through Android's share sheet;
  • a report for a doctor, as a PDF or, if you switch on With files, as a ZIP archive: the PDF, the original files attached to the lab results, attended visits and courses of medicine in the sections you ticked and the period it covers — an examination's file, a lab sheet or an allergy panel among them, each copied whole — and a list linking each file to its entry and date. A file the phone can no longer open is left out and named in that list as unreadable.

    Some of it is on every report: the period it covers, the kind of doctor it is for if you picked one, the country whose units and date format it uses, the time zone your phone is set to, your age, sex and height, and your allergies — what you told the app, or that you were never tested, and, if you saved one, your newest allergy panel: the day of the test, how many allergens it tested and how many were positive, and each positive one by its code and name, with its class and its specific IgE. The heading for your diagnoses is on every report too, but what you wrote under it goes in only if you tick it; otherwise the report says that you left it out. Everything else goes in only if you tick it, section by section. What you can tick is: your weight and BMI; the scale’s estimates of body fat, muscle and water; your waist, hip and chest measurements; your pulse and its variability, blood pressure, blood oxygen and body temperature against the reference bands this app applies; your laboratory results, and what your diary, medicines and supplements held in the weeks before each test; your medicines by name and dose, when each course started and ended, and how many doses you marked as taken; your sleep, activity and food, and the vitamins and minerals worked out from the food you logged and the doses you marked; your mood; what you logged about alcohol and tobacco; what you counted in a bathroom; the diagnoses, operations, vaccinations and contraception method you wrote down; visits you marked as attended, with the doctor’s name, the place and your note; where it hurt and how often, by area of the pain drawing, with the scores, the words and the days you marked a painkiller; the hours you tracked with the work timer, by week and by day, counted against your own usual days off and long-day setting; on a profile set to female, your pregnancy status and your menstrual cycle, including when your last period started, how long your cycles run, and the flow, spotting, ovulation tests and other observations you recorded; and, last, the figures from those sections that changed between the start of the period and its end, side by side.

    Sexual activity is never in it unless you put it there. It is not part of any doctor’s preset, “select all” does not reach it, and it goes in only after you answer a question that says what would be sent. You choose then whether to send the counts alone, or those with the individual dates. Before the report is saved or shared, a summary screen states in words whether sexual activity is included or not. Nothing about anybody else is recorded or sent.

    Nor are the symptoms you ticked on an intimate record — pain, bleeding, dryness, difficulty — unless you put them there. They have a question of their own, which no preset or “select all” reaches; what goes is which of them you ticked, never how often, on which days or anything about protection. Because they are ticked while logging sex or masturbation, a symptom on the sheet does say that one of them happened, and the question says so.

    Morning check-ins are never in it unless you put them there either, and only on a profile set to male. They have a question of their own, separate from the one about sexual activity — answering one does not include the other — and no preset or “select all” reaches them. What goes is how many mornings you answered, how often each answer was given and when you usually woke; a chart of the share answered firm or partly, week by week; and, when the report compares the start of the period with its end, that share in the first stretch beside the last, which may be set beside your sleep over the same months. No single morning is listed with its date. When they are included, the summary screen says so on a line of its own.

    The report says on its face that it is a patient-generated summary, compiled by you from your own records; that measurements may come from connected apps or devices and have not been clinically verified; and that the diagnoses, allergies, operations, vaccinations, pregnancy status and contraception on it are your own statements as you wrote them down, not coded or confirmed by a clinician. A reference band printed beside a value is one this app applies from published population guidance; it is not a laboratory’s own range for your sample, and the report says so;
  • progress photographs — all of them at once, never a selection: the one export button sends the whole gallery. A single picture leaves only if you tap it open, which hands that one file to whatever app your phone views pictures with;
  • a medicine photograph — one at a time, only if you tap it open, which hands that one file to whatever app your phone views pictures with;
  • a walk photograph — one at a time, only if you tap it open, which hands that one file to whatever app your phone views pictures with;
  • an attached file or a laboratory camera photograph — one at a time, only if you tap it open on a lab result, an examination, a medicine, an appointment or in a chat, which hands that one file to whatever app your phone opens that kind of file with;
  • a workout template, as a file another phone with the app can import — the whole template, its photograph inside it — handed through Android's share sheet to an app you pick, with the template written out in words beside it for anyone without the app;
  • an achievement card, as an image;
  • a sensor trace, as CSV, if you have been recording one;
  • a diagnostics report — see §9.

A backup is spooled through a temporary file in the app's private cache on its way out, and that spool goes as soon as the copy is across. A route, a sensor trace and a report you save through the file picker are written straight into the file you chose. A report, an achievement card, a route or a workout template you share is written into the app's private cache first, because sharing means handing another app a file; progress photographs are shared from where they already live, and the diagnostics report is text rather than a file. A shared report, route or workout template is removed the next time you open the app once the file is an hour old, and in any case the next time the app is started afresh; sharing a new report, as a PDF or as an archive, removes the last one sooner; one card is kept for each medal you have shared — up to five, each replaced only when you share that same medal again. A backup file you import is copied into the same cache as it is — still sealed, if it was sealed — and deleted when the import ends, when you leave the page without giving its password, or, if Android closed the app halfway, the next time the app is started afresh. All of them go when you clear the app's cache.

8.2 Copies that keep being made

  • The automatic daily copy, if you switch it on. You choose a folder once through Android's own folder picker; after that the app writes one complete copy of your record into it every day, keeps the seven newest days of its own copies and deletes its older ones — copies another installation wrote into the folder are left alone — and goes on doing so with the app closed until you switch it off. These copies are plain, unencrypted JSON, and they include your progress, medicine, walk and workout-template photographs and the laboratory camera photographs your results still name. They cannot be password-sealed: the app never stores your password, so there is nothing to seal a file with at three in the morning. If the folder you chose belongs to a cloud storage service, that service's app will upload a full copy of your health record every day — so choose a folder you are content to keep medical data in.
  • Health Connect, once connected — automatically, as §6 describes.
  • Moving to a new phone. When you set up a new phone from your old one, Android's own transfer copies this app's database, its settings, your progress, medicine, walk and workout-template photographs and laboratory camera photographs straight from one device to the other, over cable or Wi-Fi Direct, without passing through any server. This is deliberate: without it a lost or broken phone would take the whole record with it. Nothing is uploaded — the app excludes itself entirely from Google's cloud backup, and both the exclusion and the transfer list are in the app's data_extraction_rules.xml for anyone to read. If you switched database encryption on, the transferred database cannot be opened on the new phone, because the key stays in the old phone's keystore; move your record with an export instead.

9. What we receive

Nothing, automatically. We run no servers, and the app has no way to reach one.

The single exception is a bug report you decide to send. The About screen can produce a diagnostics summary: the app and Android version, your phone's make and model, your language and unit system, whether the app lock is on, how many days ago a backup was last made, by hand or by the automatic copy, and whether the automatic copies are on, whether Health Connect is available and how many of the permissions the app asks it for are granted, out of how many (fewer on a profile under sixteen, which is not asked about sexual activity), how many journal entries, step days, routes and progress photographs your database holds, and the random installation identifier from §2.1. It contains no entry, no measurement, no coordinate and no medicine's name. Producing it also places it on your clipboard, and the app offers to hand it to an app you pick or to a mail composer addressed to us. Nothing sends it by itself. If you do send it, we hold that message as we hold any email, for as long as it takes to answer you; write to legal@pwn-all.com to have it deleted.

Nor does the app send Android's system log. When something fails — a photograph that could not be stored, a reading the chat's model could not finish — it writes a line there saying so, with the error Android gave, which can name a file: a progress photograph's file is named after the moment it was taken. No entry, measurement or medicine's name is written there. Other apps cannot read the log; it leaves the phone only in a bug report made with Android's developer options or over a USB cable.

10. Notifications and widgets

An ongoing notification shows the day's step total and, while a route is recording or paused, which of the two and the distance so far. Android requires it while the step counter runs; it can be silenced through the step-counting notification channel in Android Settings.

Reminders are on for a new profile and can be switched off in the app's profile settings. They are: a step-goal nudge, an inactivity nudge, an evening nudge if nothing has been logged, an occasional note about your average daily steps, a nudge to eat, a nudge to drink, a medicine-dose reminder, a doctor's-appointment reminder, and — on the morning of a day you planned a workout template for — Workout today, once. While the work timer runs there is one more: after ten hours it asks whether you are still working, with a Stop button, and at sixteen it stops itself and says so. Neither names anything but the timer. With the Work screen's Start by location switched on, an automatic start or stop of the work timer is reported in a notification with an Undo button, on a quiet channel of its own, Automatic work timer, so that muting the reminders does not take the Undo away with them; it gives the time, never the place. The Undo on a start holds until the end of that day while the session is still the one the app started, and the Undo on a stop for an hour while the timer stays stopped; after that the notice goes by itself. While a check runs, Android may show a notification saying that the app is checking whether you are at work, on a channel of its own at the lowest importance, Workplace checks; it goes when the check ends, at most half a minute later, and it names neither the place nor the time.

The nudges to eat and to drink are counted from the clock, not read out of what you logged: four hours after the last meal you recorded or the last such nudge, two hours after the last water or its own last nudge, and never counting from earlier than nine in the morning — something recorded before nine does not bring the nudge forward, and on a day with neither the count simply starts at nine. Both keep to the app's waking hours — nothing between ten at night and nine in the morning — and both say only that it may be time; neither names or reads anything.

Medicine and appointment reminders never name what they are about. A dose reminder gives the time and, where it matters, whether to take the dose with food — never the medicine. Doses that fall due at the same time with the same instruction share one reminder, which says that there are several and nothing else about them. An appointment reminder gives the date and the time — never the doctor or the specialty. They do still reveal that you take scheduled medication or have an appointment, and they appear on a lock screen unless your phone is set to hide sensitive notification content; neither is held back during the app's quiet hours, so either can arrive at night. The workout reminder is different: it names the templates planned for the day, as you named them, and like the nudges it keeps to the app's waking hours.

A dose reminder rings as an alarm, and this is on unless you turn it off. It uses your phone's alarm tone at alarm volume, vibrates, and goes on sounding until you stop it or a minute passes. Where Android allows it — see §4 — the same reminder also turns the screen on and fills it, over your lock screen, with a panel carrying I took it, Stop sound and Open app. That panel says exactly what the notification says, the time and where it matters the food instruction, and never the medicine; but anyone within sight or earshot of the phone learns that a dose is due, at whatever hour it falls. I took it — on the panel, and on the notification itself, which also carries Later — marks that dose, or every dose a shared reminder stands for, as taken without opening the app or passing the app lock, as the dose widget's Taken does; Later puts the reminder off for a quarter of an hour, unless the dose would be let go before then (§5). Stopping the sound does not mark the dose as taken, and Open app still goes through the app lock if you have one. The switch is Medicine alarms, in the app's profile settings beside the reminders switch; with it off the dose is an ordinary notification again.

With a Screen time reminder switched on (§5) — both are off until you switch them on, and neither depends on the reminders switch above — there are two more, on a channel of their own, Screen time breaks. After a sitting in the social and feed apps that has run for the time you set without a break, one names the app the sitting was mostly in and how many minutes it has lasted; when the day's total in those apps passes the limit you set, the other gives that total. Neither reads anything from your journal, and like any notification they show on a lock screen unless your phone is set to hide sensitive notification content.

These sit on four separate channels in Android Settings → Apps → PWN.Health → Notifications, and you can mute any one of them without losing the rest: Medicine alarms is the ringing dose reminder, Dose reminder is the appointment reminder and the dose reminder when the alarm is switched off or its own channel is muted, Screen time breaks is the two Screen time reminders, and Reminders is everything else. Muting the first does not mute the second, and neither touches the step counter's own channel.

Of the app's health-advice topics, only the activity one is ever sent as a notification. Advice about sleep, mood, the cycle, energy, hydration, blood results, bowel habits, tobacco or intimate health is shown inside the app alone and is never posted where a lock screen could show it. The nudges to eat and to drink above are not advice and read nothing you wrote: they are worked out from the clock and from when a row of that kind was last recorded, never from what it says.

Home-screen widgets sit outside the app lock, and this is worth knowing before you place one. A widget is drawn by your launcher, which is separate software; with the app lock on, a widget still shows what it shows — today's steps against your goal; the water you drank against your target, which the app works out from your latest weight, 30 ml a kilogram within limits, so that the target can give that weight away; cigarettes today; a mood prompt, or how long until the next mood can be logged; whether a route is recording or paused, with its distance and moving time; whether the work timer is running or paused, since when, its time so far and today's hours; last night's sleep, from when to when; your latest weight and how far it has moved in thirty days; what you have eaten today against the day's allowance; the week's steps; your level, XP and daily streak; the next dose of the day, by the medicine's name and amount, with a Taken button; the day of your cycle, whether a period is running, and when the next period is expected; and on the quick-entry widget the icons you chose for it, which may include sex life or the cycle — and a tap on one still records a drink of water, a mood, a cigarette or a dose taken without asking for your fingerprint or PIN. Route recording can be started and stopped from the track widget the same way, and the work timer from its own. A tap on an icon of the quick-entry widget, or on the shortcuts widget, only opens the app at that entry, through the app lock. Block screenshots applies to the app's own windows and does not hide a widget from a screenshot of your home screen. If you do not want that visible to whoever can see your phone, do not place the widget.

11. Storage, retention and deletion

Your record lives in the app's private storage for as long as you keep it. We set no retention period, because we hold nothing to retain: what is kept is kept by you, on your phone, and goes when you delete it.

You can delete individual entries inside the app — a progress photograph goes with the × on its thumbnail, and the file goes with the row; a medicine photograph goes with its course, or when you remove it and save; a walk photograph goes with the × beside it on the walk page; a recorded route goes with the bin beside its name on that page, and its points and photographs go with it; a workout template's photograph goes with the template, or when you remove it and save, and the dates the template was planned for go with it too; a laboratory camera photograph goes with the result it belongs to, and one no saved result names at a later fresh start of the app (§3) — or erase everything at once via Android Settings → Apps → PWN.Health → Clear data, or by uninstalling the app. Either takes the photographs along with the database, and both remove any quarantined copy of the database the app is holding (see below).

Five things a deletion inside the app does not reach:

  • Deleting a chat removes its words and answers, not the entries it made. A pain, a mood, a dose or a course it saved stays until you delete it on its own page; while a chat is open, Undo on a saved entry deletes that entry the way its own page would. A chat you deleted comes back if you import a backup written before you deleted it.
  • A marker of the deletion stays. When you delete an entry of your journal, the app keeps a small record that an entry with that identity was deleted. For an entry made in this app the identity is only its number, with the day for a period entry; for one that came through Health Connect, it names the app that wrote the record and the record's own identifier; for one read out of an export file, it names the app the file came from, the kind of record and its date and time, and, for a meal, which meal and the food's name, or, for a workout, the exercise's name. It holds no measured value, and exists only so that something you deleted is not read straight back in and restored: from a copy sitting in Health Connect, from an export file you hand the app a second time, or from a backup. A backup written by this installation of the app brings back no entry you deleted after writing it. A backup from any other installation — the phone you moved from by Android's transfer, and this phone's own from before the app was installed afresh or its database had to start over, among them — is held back only for entries that came from Health Connect or an export file to begin with, and an entry logged by hand in it comes back. A recorded route you delete leaves a marker too: the moment its recording started, and nothing else about it. A backup written before the deletion does not bring that route back, whichever installation wrote it; and the marker goes into the backups you export afterwards, so an installation that has imported one of those keeps the route out as well. Nothing else leaves a marker: a course of medicine, an appointment, your medical history, a workout template or a chat comes back if you import a backup written before you deleted it. These markers go with Clear data and with the app, and they are included in backup files you export.
  • Health Connect has its own rules. Deleting a record the app itself published withdraws it there too, but only at the next exchange. The exception is what an earlier installation of the app published and a reinstall did not read back — anything dated more than 365 days ago, and the few kinds it never reads back (a planned workout, a workout's distance, climb, speed, power, cadence and active energy, a day's total energy): that stays in Health Connect until you delete it there — but for one whose route, workout or plan a backup has brought back into this installation, which is published again and takes the earlier copy's place. Deleting a record another app wrote removes it only from PWN.Health — Health Connect does not let one app delete another's data, so it has to be deleted in the app that wrote it, or in Health Connect. And uninstalling PWN.Health leaves what it published standing in Health Connect: a reinstall will read those records back.
  • Files that have already left. The automatic daily copies live in the folder you chose, outside the app, and survive both Clear data and uninstalling; delete them where they are. So do exports you saved, files you shared, and any copy Android moved to a new phone during setup.
  • Existing files you picked as attachments were never the app's to delete — they still belong to the app you picked them from. What the app gives up is its permission to read one, once no saved entry names it: at once when you delete the lab result, allergy panel or examination it is on, and otherwise at a later fresh start of the app (§2). While a copy of the database that was set aside (see below) is on the phone, that start gives up nothing, because the copy may still name the file.

One more thing the app keeps on purpose: if the database is ever damaged, arrives from another phone locked to that phone's keystore, or cannot be opened because this phone's keystore went on not answering, the app moves the file aside inside its own private storage rather than deleting it, so that a record is never destroyed for being unreadable. Each kind of copy is shown on the profile page, with a button that discards it. A damaged one can also be saved from there as a file of your choosing, which is then outside the app like any export; one that this phone's own key opens can be opened again, at the next start, in place of the record in use, which is then set aside in its turn. Clear data and uninstalling remove them all.

12. Protecting what is on the phone

Three separate protections, all off until you switch them on:

  • App lock — asks for your device's PIN, or for a fingerprint or a face where the phone itself rates it a strong biometric, before showing the app. It covers the app's own screens; it does not cover home-screen widgets (see §10), and it does not cover the medicine alarm, which is meant to be read without unlocking. That panel carries the dose's time, the food instruction where there is one, whether a meal has been logged where the instruction turns on it, and that the dose is not yet marked as taken — never the medicine, and nothing else from your record. Its I took it marks that dose as taken from there, without the lock (§10). Opening the app from it goes through the lock like any other way in. It asks only while the phone has a screen lock of its own: on a phone without one there is nothing to ask for, and the app opens without asking until a screen lock is set again. Android asks for the screen lock before it lets it be removed.
  • Block screenshots — hides the app's own window from screenshots and from the recent-apps preview. The app lock brings it along: while the lock is on, this is on whatever its own switch says. It does not reach the medicine alarm panel, which is kept out of the recent-apps list instead.
  • Database encryption — rewrites the database under SQLCipher with AES-256. Its key is kept wrapped by a second key in this phone's keystore, which never leaves the phone. This is the protection against the storage being read away from the phone: a forensic image, a partition backup. It does not stop software running with root on the phone itself: the keystore key is not tied to your screen lock — the step counter and the widgets have to open the database with nobody there to unlock it — so such software can ask for it the way the app does. Read what the switch says before turning it on: because that key never leaves this phone, an encrypted database cannot be opened after a device-to-device transfer, and your record then has to travel as an export instead. It covers the database file — not your progress, medicine, walk, workout-template or laboratory camera photographs, and not the app's settings.

These address someone else getting hold of your phone or its storage. The other thing worth guarding is a file you have already exported: that is why a manual backup can be sealed with a passphrase, and why the automatic daily copy — which cannot be sealed — deserves a folder you have thought about.

13. Legal basis, and your rights

Where the EU or UK GDPR applies to you, the basis for processing health data in this app is your explicit consent under Article 9(2)(a), given by granting the relevant Android permission or Health Connect access and by choosing to record what you record. You may withdraw it at any time by revoking that permission or deleting the data; withdrawal does not affect what was done before it.

Your rights are unusual here in that almost all of them are exercised on the device, because we hold nothing to act on:

  • Access and portability — export your record as a backup file, which contains all of it in a readable format;
  • Rectification — edit any entry in the app;
  • Erasure — delete the entry, or Clear data, or uninstall (with the limits in §11);
  • Restriction and objection — revoke the Android permission or the Health Connect grant that feeds the feature, or switch the feature off;
  • Anything concerning a message you sent us — write to legal@pwn-all.com, which is the only route that is not self-service, because a bug report you emailed is the only thing we could ever hold.

You have the right to complain to a supervisory authority: in the EU, the authority in your Member State; in the UK, the Information Commissioner's Office; and in the United Arab Emirates, where we are established, the UAE Data Office.

No automated decision producing legal or similarly significant effects is made about you. The app's estimates, bands and verdicts — a wellbeing score, a cycle prediction, whether a laboratory result sits inside its reference band — are calculations made on your phone and shown to you. Nobody else sees them, and they are not profiling for anybody's benefit but yours. Nor is what the chat's model reads in a message, in a version that carries one, a decision about you: it only chooses among the chat's fixed questions and buttons, and only your taps answer them (§2.2).

Categories of recipient, for completeness: the camera app that takes a photograph, any app you hand a file to, the storage service behind a folder you chose, any app you have authorised in Health Connect, and PWN-ALL itself if you send a diagnostics report. Every one of those is a choice you make.

14. Age

PWN.Health is for every age. Before it opens any of its features, the app asks for your date of birth on a screen with nothing filled in and no age limit mentioned. That date is stored on the device with the rest of your profile and, like everything else here, never leaves it by itself.

What the date decides is which parts of the app exist for you. Under 18 there is no alcohol or tobacco logging: no screen, no button in the add sheet, and the tobacco home-screen widget is switched off so the launcher does not offer it. Under 16 there is no sexual-activity logging, on the same terms — removed, not locked, because a door that is closed but visible tells a child exactly which lie to tell. Everything else — steps, food, sleep, mood, the cycle, lab results, the medicine cabinet — is the same app at every age, and the guidance the app gives is banded by age where medicine bands it by age.

Because the app asks for a date of birth, it knows the age of the person using it. There is no minimum: a child can use PWN.Health, and their record stays on their own phone under exactly the same terms as anyone else's. Where a child is below the age at which their country lets them consent for themselves — between 13 and 16 across the EU and the UK — that consent should come from a parent or guardian, and we would expect the parent or guardian to be the one who decides the app goes on the phone.

16. Not medical advice
